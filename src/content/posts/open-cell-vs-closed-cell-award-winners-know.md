Ask a genuinely skilled spray foam applicator when to use open-cell versus closed-cell foam, and you'll get a specific, assembly-driven answer — not a one-size-fits-all default. That specificity is part of what separates competition-caliber knowledge from a generic sales pitch.

## It's Not About Which Is "Better"

Open-cell and closed-cell foam serve different purposes: open-cell is lighter, more vapor-permeable, and lower cost; closed-cell is denser, higher R-value per inch, and — at sufficient thickness — functions as a vapor retarder with real structural rigidity. Neither is universally superior; the right choice depends entirely on the specific assembly and climate.

## Reading the Assembly Correctly

A skilled applicator evaluates the specific wall or roof assembly — climate zone, vapor-drive direction, cavity depth, whether structural rigidity is needed — before defaulting to a product. Below-grade applications, tight roof-deck cavities, and flood-prone areas generally call for closed-cell; standard wall cavities in mixed climates often don't need it.

## Why This Judgment Shows Up in Competition Settings Too

Even at live competitions, the underlying material knowledge matters — understanding how each chemistry behaves under application conditions (temperature, humidity, substrate) affects how consistently a competitor can hit target thickness and coverage. The same product knowledge that wins a competition informs the material recommendation you should be getting on a real quote.

## A Red Flag Worth Knowing

If a contractor recommends the same product for every job regardless of assembly — always closed-cell, or always the cheaper open-cell — that's a reasonable signal they're applying inventory preference rather than genuine assembly-specific judgment. A specific, reasoned explanation of why a particular product fits your particular situation is what real expertise sounds like.

## Bringing This Into Your Own Quote Conversation

Ask directly: "Why closed-cell (or open-cell) for my specific project?" A confident, assembly-specific answer referencing your climate, cavity type, or vapor-control needs is a strong positive signal — closer to the kind of technical fluency the real competitions are ultimately testing for.
